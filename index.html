Why the Compass Lies

The magnetic compass is the only direction-seeking instrument in the airplane, and it is wrong almost all of the time. Chapter 8 lists six ways it goes wrong. Below, each one is a working instrument you can drive — move the controls and watch the needle disagree with reality.

Six errors, but only three reasons

The list is easier to hold onto once you notice the six errors aren't six separate facts. They come from three different things going wrong, and the last reason alone accounts for half the list.

North isn't where the map says

The Earth's magnetic pole isn't at the geographic pole, so the needle and your chart's north disagree. Nothing is broken — you just have to do the arithmetic.

→ Variation

Your airplane is magnetic

Radios, wiring, engine steel and speakers all make their own magnetic fields, and the compass is sitting right in the middle of them.

→ Deviation

The field points down, not along

Away from the equator the field dives into the ground and drags the card down with it. That single fact causes the entire rest of the list — it's the one worth actually understanding.

Error 1 · North isn't where the map says

Variation

True north is the geographic pole your chart is drawn around. Magnetic north is where the needle actually points, roughly 1,200 miles away. Variation is the angle between them — and it depends entirely on where you're standing.

The dashed magenta lines are isogonic lines — every point on one has the same variation. The bold one through the middle is the agonic line, where variation is zero and the compass tells the truth for once.

This is the one error on the list you correct on the ground with arithmetic, not in the air with technique. It doesn't care about your heading, your speed, or your bank — only your position.

Error 2 · Your airplane is magnetic

Deviation

Metal and current in the airframe bend the field before it ever reaches the compass. Unlike variation, deviation is different on every heading — because as the airplane turns, its own magnetic junk swings around relative to magnetic north.

Drag the handheld radio anywhere in the cockpit — or focus it and use the arrow keys. Watch the needle chase it, and watch the correction card rebuild itself.

Compass correction card

This is the card screwed to the panel under the compass in every airplane you'll fly. A mechanic swings the compass on a compass rose, measures the error every 30°, and writes down what to steer to actually get the heading you want. It is only valid for that airplane, with that equipment, in that configuration.

Error 3 · The field points down — this one causes the rest

Magnetic dip

Lines of flux leave the Earth near the south pole and re-enter near the north pole. Only at the magnetic equator do they run flat along the surface. Everywhere else they angle into the ground, and the compass card — free to tip on its pivot — tries to follow them down.

The blue arrow is the horizontal part of the field — the only part that can actually turn the card toward north. The magenta arrow is the vertical part, which does nothing useful and causes errors 4, 5 and 6.

The key link

Dip isn't really a reading error by itself — it's the mechanism. Because the card hangs tipped, anything that pushes the airplane around (banking, accelerating, turbulence) shoves the tipped card sideways and spins it. Set the slider to 0° and the next two errors vanish completely.

Error 4 · Caused by dip

Northerly turning error

Roll into a bank and the tipped card gets flung outward by the turn. Near north the compass lags behind the airplane — it briefly shows a turn the wrong way. Near south it races ahead. On east and west it's honest.

The magenta number under the lubber line is what you'd read. The blue tick is where the card should be sitting. The shaded wedge between them is the error.

UNOS

Undershoot North, Overshoot South. Rolling out on a northerly heading, stop the turn early; on a southerly heading, carry it past. How much? Roughly your latitude in degrees — about 40° here.

Error 5 · Also caused by dip

Acceleration error

Same tipped card, different shove. Change speed on an east or west heading and the card's weight lags behind, twisting it into a turn that isn't happening. On north and south headings there's nothing to twist.

Nothing here is turning. The airplane's wings are level and the heading is constant — the card is moving purely because the airspeed is changing.

ANDS

Accelerate North, Decelerate South. Speed up and the compass swings toward north; slow down and it swings toward south — no matter which of the two headings you're on. Notice the curve is the exact mirror of the last one: this error peaks where turning error is zero.

Error 6 · All of the above, at once

Oscillation

Turbulence doesn't introduce a new kind of error — it just applies errors 4 and 5 repeatedly, in random directions, several times a second. The card never settles, and the number under the lubber line becomes something you have to average by eye.

The faint magenta band is the full sweep the card is covering. Your eye has to find the middle of it.

This is why you don't chase the compass. In anything but smooth air you set the heading indicator against the compass during straight, level, unaccelerated flight — the one condition where every error on this page except variation and deviation is guaranteed to be zero — and then fly the heading indicator instead.

Follows the magnetic compass section of the FAA Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge (FAA-H-8083-25), Chapter 8, Flight Instruments.

The instruments above are teaching models, not avionics. They reproduce the shape, sign and rough magnitude of each error — where it peaks, where it vanishes, which way it swings — using the standard rules of thumb. Real deviation depends on the specific airframe, and real lead/lag depends on bank and rate as well as latitude. Use them to build the intuition, then trust the correction card in the airplane.
